# **Strategic Capitalization and Client Acquisition for Texas AI Consulting: A 2026 Market Analysis**

## **Executive Strategic Overview**

The establishment of Texas AI Consulting in January 2026 occurs at a pivotal moment in the economic and legislative history of Texas. The convergence of the 89th Legislative Session’s aggressive modernization policies, the overhaul of the state's tax incentive structures under Subchapter T, and the nascent enforcement of the Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (TRAIGA) creates a tripartite opportunity structure for the firm. For a single-member, Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business (SDVOSB), the pathway to capitalization is not merely about securing direct funding but about leveraging the complex apparatus of government incentives to subsidize client acquisition.

The premise of "using government money," as popularized by financial commentators like Codie Sanchez, is often misunderstood as a direct transfer of liquidity from the treasury to the startup. In the context of professional services and high-technology consulting, the reality is far more nuanced and potentially more lucrative. The government does not simply fund the startup; it funds the *client’s* engagement with the startup. By mastering the intricate web of the Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 162 deductions, the Texas Research and Development (R\&D) Franchise Tax Credit, and the Texas Workforce Commission (TWC) grant infrastructure, Texas AI Consulting can effectively discount its services by 20% to 100% at no cost to its own bottom line.

This report serves as a comprehensive operational dossier. It deconstructs the mechanisms of state and federal finance to provide an actionable roadmap for funding operations and, critically, for framing the firm’s value proposition to prospective clients. The analysis moves beyond surface-level advice to explore the statutory definitions, compliance mechanisms, and strategic negotiations required to monetize these incentives in the 2026 fiscal environment.

**1\. The Legislative and Economic Landscape of Texas in 2026**

To understand how to fund Texas AI Consulting and incentivize its clients, one must first understand the terrain on which these transactions occur. The Texas economy of 2026 is defined by a deliberate shift from passive tax breaks to active, performance-based incentives designed to foster technological sovereignty.

### **1.1 The Shift from Chapter 313 to Subchapter T**

For decades, Texas relied on Chapter 313 agreements to lure large capital investments. With the expiration of that program and the repeal of the previous R\&D credit (Subchapter M), the state faced a potential innovation vacuum. The 89th Legislature responded with the enactment of **Subchapter T**, effective January 1, 2026\. This is not merely a tax cut; it is a structural realignment of the state's franchise tax system to prioritize research and development activities.1

For a specialized consultancy like Texas AI Consulting, this shift is fundamental. The new Subchapter T credit is permanent, removing the uncertainty that plagued previous incentives. It explicitly aligns with federal definitions of "qualified research," which includes the development of software and algorithmic models.3 This legislative change transforms the firm's services from an operational expense into a tax-advantaged asset for potential clients. When Texas AI Consulting engages in the experimental design of AI systems for a client, the fees paid for that service are no longer just costs; they become the basis for a lucrative tax credit that can now be refunded even to pre-profit entities.4

### **1.2 The Regulatory Awakening: HB 149 and TRAIGA**

Simultaneously, the regulatory environment has tightened. The passage of House Bill 149, known as the **Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (TRAIGA)**, effective January 1, 2026, imposes new liabilities on businesses deploying AI.5 This legislation prohibits "algorithmic discrimination" and mandates transparency in AI-consumer interactions. While regulation often imposes costs, for a consulting firm, it creates a market.

The "compliance market" is now a primary revenue vector. Clients are no longer just looking for AI efficiency; they are seeking insulation from the $12,000 to $200,000 civil penalties established by TRAIGA.6 Texas AI Consulting can position itself not just as a technology implementer but as a risk mitigator, helping clients navigate the state's new "Sandbox Program" managed by the Department of Information Resources (DIR). This program allows for the testing of AI systems under a liability shield, a feature that effectively subsidizes innovation risk.6

### **1.3 The Veteran Entrepreneurship Ecosystem**

The third pillar of this landscape is the state’s reinforced commitment to veteran entrepreneurship. The Texas Veterans Commission (TVC) and the Comptroller’s office have streamlined the **VetHUB** certification process, aiming to increase the utilization of SDVOSBs in state contracting. With statutory goals suggesting that 21.1% of commodities contracts and 26% of "other services" contracts go to Historically Underutilized Businesses (HUBs), the state government itself becomes a viable anchor client.7 The 2026 fiscal landscape thus presents a scenario where the government provides the tax code to incentivize private clients, the regulatory framework to drive demand for compliance services, and the procurement mandates to serve as a direct customer.

**2\. The Client Incentive Strategy: The Business Education Deduction**

The first and most immediate mechanism for incentivizing clients is the leveraging of the **Business Education Deduction**. The user query specifically references incentivizing clients by informing them of this deduction. While the popular discourse, such as that by Codie Sanchez, frames this as a simple "write-off," the execution in 2026 requires a sophisticated understanding of IRS regulations, particularly following the discontinuation of the physical Publication 535 booklet.

### **2.1 Deconstructing IRC §162 and Regulation §1.162-5**

The statutory authority for deducting education expenses resides in **Internal Revenue Code (IRC) §162**, which governs "Trade or Business Expenses." Specifically, **Treasury Regulation §1.162-5** outlines the distinct criteria under which education expenses are deductible.8 For Texas AI Consulting, the sales strategy hinges on accurately classifying its services within these definitions.

To be deductible, the education (i.e., the training provided by Texas AI Consulting) must meet one of two positive tests:

1. **Maintains or Improves Skills:** The education must maintain or improve skills required by the individual in their employment or other trade or business.  
2. **Meets Employer or Legal Requirements:** The education must meet the express requirements of the individual's employer, or the requirements of applicable law or regulations, imposed as a condition to the retention of an established employment relationship, status, or rate of compensation.9

However, the strategy faces two critical "negative tests" or disqualifiers. The deduction is disallowed if:

1. The education is required to meet the *minimum* educational requirements for qualification in the taxpayer's employment.  
2. The education qualifies the taxpayer for a *new* trade or business.9

### **2.2 Strategic Framing: "Upskilling" vs. "Retraining"**

The nuance for Texas AI Consulting lies in the definition of a "new trade or business." In the context of AI, this distinction is vital. If the firm pitches a comprehensive "AI Bootcamp" that promises to turn a client's administrative assistants into "Data Scientists," the IRS could argue that this qualifies the employees for a new trade, rendering the expense non-deductible under the education rules (though potentially amortizable as a startup cost).

Instead, the engagement must be framed as **"Technological Upskilling for Existing Roles."** The narrative presented to the client’s CFO should be:

"We are not training your staff to change careers. We are providing 'Professional Development in Advanced Computation' to maintain their efficiency in their *current* roles as market conditions evolve."

This framing aligns directly with the "Maintain or Improve Skills" test. As AI becomes ubiquitous, proficiency in Large Language Models (LLMs) and automated workflows is becoming a standard requirement for retention of status in many fields, further reinforcing the deductibility argument.12

### **2.3 The Invoice as a Compliance Document**

To facilitate this deduction for the client, Texas AI Consulting must structure its invoicing and proposals to mirror the language of the tax code. A generic invoice for "Consulting Services" is ambiguous. An optimized invoice should itemize components that are specifically deductible under different facets of the code.

| Invoice Line Item | Tax Treatment Classification | Client Benefit Narrative |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **"Technical Staff Training Seminar"** | **Education Expense (Reg §1.162-5)** | Immediately deductible as an ordinary and necessary business expense to improve employee skills. |
| **"Instructional Materials & Licenses"** | **Supplies (IRC §162)** | Deductible as business supplies necessary for the training engagement. |
| **"Executive Coaching: AI Strategy"** | **Professional Services** | Deductible as a management consulting fee, separate from "education" but fully expensable. |
| **"Employee AI Literacy Workshop"** | **Working Condition Fringe (IRC §132)** | Excluded from employee's taxable income; fully deductible by the employer.13 |

### **2.4 The §174 Amortization Trap**

A sophisticated client may raise the issue of **IRC Section 174**, which mandates the capitalization and five-year amortization of "specified research or experimental expenditures," including software development.14 This is a less favorable tax treatment than immediate expensing.

By emphasizing the **educational** and **consulting** nature of the engagement rather than the **software development** aspect, Texas AI Consulting helps the client avoid the Section 174 trap. If the firm builds a custom AI tool, that cost might need to be amortized. If the firm *teaches* the client how to prompt an existing tool to achieve the same result, that cost is an immediate deduction. This "Expense vs. Capitalize" arbitrage is a powerful sales tool. The pitch becomes: "Don't buy the software and depreciate it over five years; buy our training and write it off today."

**3\. The Texas Research and Development Tax Credit (Subchapter T)**

While the federal education deduction is a valuable incentive, the newly overhauled **Texas R\&D Franchise Tax Credit (Subchapter T)** represents a far more significant financial opportunity for clients. Effective January 1, 2026, this credit effectively allows the state government to subsidize the client's decision to hire Texas AI Consulting.

### **3.1 The 2026 Legislative Overhaul**

The 89th Legislature's enactment of Subchapter T was a direct response to the need for a permanent, robust incentive structure following the expiration of Chapter 313\. The new law, Senate Bill 2206, increases the franchise tax credit rate and aligns the definition of "Qualified Research Expense" (QRE) directly with federal Line 48 of IRS Form 6765\.3

The critical feature for Texas AI Consulting is the treatment of **"Contract Research Expenses."** Under the federal rules adopted by Texas, a business does not need to perform R\&D in-house to claim the credit. They can hire a third-party contractor—such as Texas AI Consulting—to perform the research on their behalf. The client can then include **65% of the fees paid to the contractor** as Qualified Research Expenses (QREs) in their credit calculation.15

### **3.2 Calculating the Incentive: The 65% Rule**

This mechanism effectively discounts the firm’s services. When a client pays Texas AI Consulting $100,000 for a project that meets the definition of qualified research, the client receives a tax credit basis of $65,000.

With the 2026 rate increase, the standard credit is now **8.722%** of the QRE (assuming the Alternative Simplified Credit method or similar state calculation base).

* **Mathematical Impact:**  
  * Invoice Amount: $100,000  
  * Eligible QRE (65%): $65,000  
  * Texas Tax Credit (8.722%): \~$5,669  
  * *Note:* This credit is applied directly against the Texas Franchise Tax liability, which is a significant "above the line" savings compared to a deduction.

### **3.3 Qualifying the Engagement: The Four-Part Test**

For the client to claim this credit, the work performed by Texas AI Consulting must pass the IRS "Four-Part Test," which is applied strictly under Texas regulations.17 Eliud Lamboy must structure the Statement of Work (SOW) to document compliance with these tests:

1. **Permitted Purpose:** The activity must relate to a new or improved business component (e.g., "Developing a novel predictive maintenance algorithm"). It cannot be for purely aesthetic or routine maintenance work.  
2. **Technological in Nature:** The research must rely on the principles of physical or biological science, engineering, or computer science. Using AI technologies inherently satisfies this, provided the work involves underlying technical architecture or algorithmic logic.  
3. **Elimination of Uncertainty:** This is the most critical documentation point. The SOW must explicitly state that the project is undertaken to eliminate uncertainty regarding the *capability*, *method*, or *appropriate design* of the AI solution.  
   * *SOW Language:* "Project Alpha aims to determine the technical feasibility of integrating Large Language Models into legacy SQL databases where the latency requirements are under 50ms, a capability currently unproven in the client's infrastructure."  
4. **Process of Experimentation:** The project must involve a systematic process of evaluating alternatives. Texas AI Consulting should document the "iterations," "simulations," and "testing protocols" used during the engagement.

### **3.4 Refundability: A Game Changer for Startups**

A pivotal innovation in the 2026 legislation is the introduction of **refundability** for certain entities. Previously, if a company owed no franchise tax (common for pre-revenue startups), the credit was useless. Under Subchapter T, eligible entities (often those with revenue below the 'No Tax Due' threshold or qualified veteran-owned businesses) can apply for a **refundable credit**.18

This means Texas AI Consulting can pitch to early-stage startup clients: "Even if you aren't profitable yet, hiring us can generate a cash refund from the State of Texas." This fundamentally changes the economics of the engagement for cash-strapped startups.

**4\. Workforce Subsidies: The Texas Workforce Commission (TWC)**

Beyond tax credits, the Texas Workforce Commission (TWC) manages direct grant programs that fund employee training. While typically these funds flow to the employer or a community college, a strategic partnership model allows Texas AI Consulting to position itself as the funded provider.

### **4.1 Skills for Small Business (SSB) Grant**

The **Skills for Small Business** program is targeted at private businesses with fewer than 100 employees.20

* **The Funding:** Up to **$1,800** per new employee (hired within 12 months) and **$900** per incumbent employee for training costs.22  
* **The Constraint:** The training must be provided by a public community or technical college or the Texas A\&M Engineering Extension Service (TEEX). Third-party vendors are generally excluded from direct payment under this specific program.21

**Internal Application for Texas AI Consulting:**

Eliud Lamboy can utilize this program for *his own* employees. As the firm scales, he can hire staff and send them to local colleges for certification in project management or basic coding, with TWC covering the tuition. This subsidizes his internal talent development.

### **4.2 Skills Development Fund (SDF): The Partnership Model**

The **Skills Development Fund** is the state’s primary mechanism for customized job training, offering grants up to **$500,000**.23 Unlike the SSB, the SDF allows for a partnership model that can directly fund private training vendors through a subcontracting arrangement.

**The Loophole: The "Third-Party Trainer" Provision** A private business cannot apply for SDF alone; it must partner with an "Eligible Applicant," usually a community college. However, the regulations (Texas Administrative Code Chapter 803\) allow the college to subcontract a portion of the training to a third party if the college cannot provide that specific technical instruction.24

**Operational Strategy:**

1. **Identify the End-Client:** Texas AI Consulting identifies a corporate client (e.g., a manufacturing firm in Dallas) that needs highly specialized "Generative AI for Supply Chain" training.  
2. **Forge the Consortium:** The firm approaches a local college (e.g., Dallas College or Collin College) and the client to form a consortium.  
3. **The Proposal:** The college applies for the SDF grant. In the application, Texas AI Consulting is listed as the **"Third-Party Training Provider"** for the proprietary AI portion of the curriculum.  
4. **The Split:** TWC regulations typically allow up to 50% of the training to be conducted by a third party for initial projects.24  
5. **Revenue Realization:** TWC awards the grant to the college. The college pays Texas AI Consulting for the delivery of the specialized training. The client gets the training for free (grant-funded).

**Key Contacts and Regional Liaisons:**

To execute this, Texas AI Consulting must work through the TWC’s **Employer Engagement and Community Outreach (EECO)** team. The regional business liaisons are the gatekeepers who facilitate these partnerships.

**5\. The Veteran Advantage: VetHUB and Procurement**

For a Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business (SDVOSB), the "veteran status" is a tangible asset in the government procurement marketplace. The user’s awareness of **VetHUB** is a starting point, but the operationalization of this certification requires moving beyond the "set-aside" mindset to a "compliance solution" mindset.

### **5.1 Beyond "Set-Asides": The Goal-Oriented Environment**

Texas does not strictly "set aside" contracts for veterans in the same way the federal government does. Instead, it operates on a "Good Faith Effort" (GFE) model with specific utilization goals. For 2026, the goals are approximately 21.1% for commodities and 23.7% to 26% for services.7

**The "Prime" Strategy:**

The largest opportunities for Texas AI Consulting are likely not in direct contracts with the state (which can be slow and bureaucratic) but in **subcontracting** with large Prime Contractors (e.g., Deloitte, Accenture, IBM) who hold massive state IT contracts.

* **The Mechanism:** When a Prime bids on a contract over $100,000, they *must* submit a **HUB Subcontracting Plan (HSP)** detailing how they will meet the state's HUB goals. If they fail to meet the goal or show good faith, their bid can be disqualified.  
* **The Value Proposition:** Texas AI Consulting solves a compliance problem for the Prime. By including Eliud Lamboy’s firm on their HSP, the Prime improves their chances of winning the contract.  
* **Action Item:** The firm must be listed on the **Centralized Master Bidders List (CMBL)** and actively market to Primes during the pre-bid phase. Attending "Spot Bid Fairs" and HUB expos is essential networking to be "scouted" by Primes.26

### **5.2 Federal Sole-Source Authority**

At the federal level, the leverage is even stronger. The SBA allows for **Sole-Source Contracts** to SDVOSBs. This means a federal Contracting Officer (CO) can award a contract directly to Texas AI Consulting without a competitive bidding process, provided:

1. The contract is below $4 million (or $7 million for manufacturing).  
2. The CO does not expect two or more SDVOSBs to submit offers (the "Rule of Two").  
3. The price is fair and reasonable.27

**Targeting Strategy:**

Texas AI Consulting should target federal agencies with a strong Texas footprint that have autonomy in contracting.

* **NASA Johnson Space Center (Houston):** High demand for AI in simulation and data processing.  
* **Army Futures Command (Austin):** Specifically focused on modernization and technology integration.  
* **Medical Centers (VA):** Potential for AI in administrative workflow optimization.

The pitch to a Federal Program Manager is: "I can solve this problem for you immediately. I am SDVOSB certified. You can sole-source this to me under FAR 19.1406, avoiding a 6-month bidding war."

**6\. The Compliance Market: Monetizing HB 149**

The **Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (HB 149\)** is a market-making piece of legislation. By imposing strict governance requirements on state agencies and businesses, it creates an immediate demand for specialized consulting services.

### **6.1 The "Compliance as a Service" Product**

The act prohibits "algorithmic discrimination" and mandates disclosure protocols.6 Most businesses in Texas are currently unprepared for these requirements. Texas AI Consulting can productize this compliance need:

* **"HB 149 Readiness Assessment":** A standardized audit of a client's current AI usage to identify potential liabilities under the new law.  
* **"Algorithmic Bias Testing":** Using technical tools to test client models for discriminatory outputs, a requirement implied by the "algorithmic discrimination" prohibition.  
* **"Disclosure Framework Implementation":** Drafting the technical and consumer-facing language required for chatbots and automated decision systems.

### **6.2 The Sandbox Advisory Service**

HB 149 creates an **AI Sandbox** within the Department of Information Resources, allowing companies to test AI systems for up to 36 months without standard regulatory authorizations.6

* **The Opportunity:** Accessing this Sandbox is a competitive advantage for clients, as it shields them from certain liabilities and accelerates time-to-market.  
* **The Service:** Texas AI Consulting can offer a "Sandbox Application & Management" service, guiding clients through the bureaucratic application process, designing the required testing protocols, and managing the reporting requirements to the DIR. This positions the firm as a gateway to innovation.

**7\. Operational Funding: Capitalizing the Startup**

While incentivizing clients is the primary revenue driver, establishing the firm’s own capital base is necessary for stability.

### **7.1 Tax Waivers and Verification**

The most immediate "funding" is cost avoidance. As a new veteran-owned business formed between 2022 and 2026, Texas AI Consulting is eligible for a **Franchise Tax Waiver** for its first five years of operation.

* **Action:** Eliud Lamboy must obtain the **Veteran Verification Letter** from the TVC immediately. This letter is then submitted to the Secretary of State and Comptroller to activate the waiver, saving thousands in annual tax liability.28

### **7.2 Grant Landscape for 2026**

Direct grants for startups are competitive, but specific pools exist for veterans:

* **Hiring Our Heroes:** Awards grants ($10k–$25k) to veteran-owned businesses. The cycle typically aligns with the academic year or early calendar year.30  
* **Texas Semiconductor Innovation Fund (TSIF):** While manufacturing-heavy, this fund (appropriated \~$948 million) includes provisions for "design" projects. If the consultancy moves into hardware-adjacent AI optimization, it may qualify for state grants under this umbrella.31  
* **SBIR Matching (Texas Technology and Innovation Program):** The 89th Legislature considered programs to match federal SBIR grants. If Texas AI Consulting wins a federal research grant (Phase I), the state may provide matching non-dilutive capital. This requires a dedicated R\&D focus rather than pure consulting.32

### **7.3 Micro-Lending and Interest Buydowns**

For working capital, **LiftFund** (a Texas CDFI) offers specific veteran loan programs with "interest buydowns," effectively reducing interest rates to as low as 2.5% or 5.5%.33 This is significantly cheaper than commercial credit and is accessible to startups with limited credit history.
